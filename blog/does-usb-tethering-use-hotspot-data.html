Does USB Tethering Use Hotspot Data on an iPhone? Yes. Here's Why.

Short answer: yes. On an iPhone, USB tethering, the Wi-Fi hotspot and Bluetooth tethering are one feature, Personal Hotspot, reached three different ways. All three draw from the same hotspot allowance. The cable changes stability and battery life. It does not change what your carrier counts.

Why the cable makes no difference

A common belief is that carriers spot tethering by watching for Wi-Fi hotspots, so a cable slips past. That is not how it works on an iPhone. When Personal Hotspot is on, iOS knows exactly which traffic belongs to other devices, because iOS is the one forwarding it. Whether that traffic arrived over Wi-Fi, USB or Bluetooth, it goes out the same way.

On many carriers the iPhone goes one step further and opens a second cellular data connection used only for shared traffic. We saw this directly while testing on T-Mobile: with Personal Hotspot on, the phone had one cellular connection for its own apps and a second one that carried only the traffic of the connected computer. The carrier does not have to guess. The phone hands tethered traffic over separately, and that side has its own allowance and its own speed limit.

You may have read that carriers detect tethering from packet details such as TTL values. Those methods exist to catch tethering that tries to hide. iPhone's built-in Personal Hotspot does not hide, so they hardly matter here.

So USB is still worth using. It does not go idle, nobody nearby can join it, there is no Wi-Fi interference, and the phone ends the session charged. It just reaches the allowance at the same point as Wi-Fi.

How to check your own hotspot usage

  1. On the iPhone, open Settings › Cellular (Mobile Data in some regions).
  2. Scroll down the list to Personal Hotspot. The number beside it is the data other devices have used through the phone. Tap it to see each device.
  3. This counter does not follow your billing month. It runs until you tap Reset Statistics at the bottom of the screen, so reset it on your billing date if you want to track the allowance.

Tether over USB for ten minutes and watch that number. It grows exactly as it does over Wi-Fi. Your carrier's app shows the figure that actually decides when you are slowed down.

What happens when the allowance runs out

The phone's own apps keep full speed. Devices on the hotspot are slowed for the rest of the billing month, commonly to around 600 kbps and on some plans to 128 kbps. Pages still load, slowly; video calls and large downloads do not really work. The carrier guides have the numbers: T-Mobile, Verizon, AT&T.

What does use the phone's regular data

The allowance applies to traffic the iPhone forwards for other devices through Personal Hotspot. Traffic that an app on the phone sends itself is ordinary phone data. A relay app builds on that difference: the computer hands its traffic to an app on the phone, and the app opens the connections.

FAQ

Does USB tethering count as hotspot data on Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile?

Yes, on all three. On an iPhone, USB tethering is Personal Hotspot over a cable, and it draws from the same hotspot allowance as the Wi-Fi hotspot.

Does Bluetooth tethering use hotspot data?

Yes. Bluetooth is the third way to reach Personal Hotspot. It is the slowest of the three and uses the same allowance.

Is USB tethering faster than the Wi-Fi hotspot?

It is more consistent, and often faster in practice because there is no Wi-Fi interference. On iPhones with a Lightning port the cable itself tops out at USB 2.0 speed, which is still far above what most cellular connections deliver.

Does USB tethering use less data than Wi-Fi?

No. The computer sends and receives the same data either way. What changes the total is what the computer does: system updates, cloud sync and video quality.

How do I see how much hotspot data I have used?

Open Settings, then Cellular, and scroll to Personal Hotspot for the iPhone's own counter. Your carrier's app shows the official figure for the billing month.

The short version

USB tethering is Personal Hotspot with a cable. It is steadier than Wi-Fi and keeps the phone charged, and it uses the same hotspot allowance. To use the phone's regular data from a computer, the traffic has to be sent by an app on the phone, which is what a proxy set up by hand or FastNet does.
